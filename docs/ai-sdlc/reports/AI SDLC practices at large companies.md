# Agents speed up code, not delivery

At the end of 2026, in AI labs as in large product companies, the coding agent has become a full PR author and a near-systematic reviewer, but no organization lets it carry a ticket to production on its own. Where the share is published, in-house background agents write between 10 and 30% of merged PRs (Uber 11% in March 2026, Doctolib 10%, Monzo about 10%, Ramp about 30%), Stripe merges more than 1,300 per week, and AI review covers more than 90% of PRs at Microsoft and of diffs at Uber; yet humans keep the merge everywhere, SRE agents investigate and propose, and deployment stays with the pipelines. Scaling across dozens of teams follows the same three-layer pattern everywhere: a platform team owns the model gateway, identity, sandboxes, telemetry and costs; a versioned base convention of skills, plugins, hooks and rules is pushed to everyone; each team adds its rules as close to its code as possible. The vendors themselves document that CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md or Copilot instructions are context the model may ignore, and that only managed settings, hooks and CI guarantee a behavior. On effect, the showcase figures (70 to 90% of code written by AI at Anthropic, 75% of new code at Google) are statements with incompatible definitions; the broadest longitudinal telemetry (DX, more than 400 companies) measures a median PR throughput gain of 7.76%, and Faros, an analytics vendor, observes larger PRs, review five times slower and more incidents per PR. Human review has become the bottleneck; auto-approval of PRs judged low-risk (Zalando 33%, Intercom 19%) only exists surrounded by heavy guardrails and is already being bypassed. For an enterprise AI SDLC framework, this evidence says that a common base convention must stay thin, that what must be guaranteed must be executed by code rather than written in Markdown, and that the flow must adapt to the size of the change and to each repository's capacity for verification.

> **How to read the figures.** Each figure is dated, sourced and qualified by its nature: *measured* (telemetry, paper with published method, randomized trial), *declared* (executive, spokesperson or company post without method), *vendor* (published by whoever sells the tool or the measurement tool: customer pages of Anthropic, OpenAI or Atlassian, DX, Faros, LinearB, Jellyfish reports, and labs' internal figures on their own agents), *perception* (survey or self-report). Natures combine ("measured, vendor"). *Secondary* flags that the primary source could not be read; *old* marks an item from before 2025. Unless stated otherwise, the date is the publication date.

## At the end of 2026, the agent opens and reviews PRs, but a human always merges

Between 2024 and the end of 2026, the center of gravity moved from in-editor completion to the agent that chains dozens of tool calls on its own and then hands back a PR. The shift is measured where it is measured best, at Anthropic: between February and August 2025, over 200,000 internal Claude Code transcripts, the maximum number of consecutive tool calls went from 9.8 to 21.2, human turns per transcript from 6.2 to 4.1, and the share of feature implementation tasks from 14.3% to 36.9% (*measured, vendor*, published 2 Dec. 2025) ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)). Winter 2025–2026 marks a common tipping point: at Uber, Claude Code usage went from 32% to 63% of developers between December 2025 and February 2026 while IDE tools plateaued (*declared*, 10 March 2026) ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-uber-uses-ai-for-development)); Spotify dates the acceleration of adoption to the release of Opus 4.5 at the end of 2025 ([Spotify, 3 June 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)); Codex became central at OpenAI around January 2026, as IDE usage declined ([Pragmatic Engineer, 15 Sept. 2026](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)). Everywhere, the movement stops at the same place: the PR reviewed and merged by a human.

### The labs show the ceiling, with in-house figures

Anthropic is the most instrumented case. Its internal study (132 respondents, 31% response rate, 53 interviews, 200,000 transcripts) shows the share of daily work done with Claude going from 28% to 59% in a year and self-reported productivity gain from 20% to 50% (*perception*), while telemetry gives **+67% merged PRs per engineer per day** (*measured, vendor*); yet more than half of respondents can fully delegate only 0 to 20% of their work ([Anthropic, 2 Dec. 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)). In January 2026, a spokesperson told Fortune that 70 to 90% of the company's code was written by AI, without a published method (*declared*) ([Fortune, 29 Jan. 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/01/29/100-percent-of-code-at-anthropic-and-openai-is-now-ai-written-boris-cherny-roon/)). The 200% increase in code output per engineer, announced at the launch of Claude Code Review on 9 March 2026 (*declared*), is precisely what saturated reviewers and motivated a multi-agent review tool run on almost all internal PRs ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/code-review)).

OpenAI describes a "software factory": the human defines the outcome, Codex gathers the context (repository documentation, Slack, Notion, Datadog, internal skills), implements, tests and watches CI; specialized review agents (cloud infrastructure, security) then run; changes are classified by risk and low-risk areas can opt in to auto-approval; after human approval, one agent per change drives deployment through feature flags; the incident agent "Sevbot" proposes mitigations that it executes only on an engineer's instruction, and the human on-call remains (*declared, reported by a third party*, 15 Sept. 2026) ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)). The adoption figures, 92% of technical staff on Codex every day and 70% more PRs per week, date from DevDay on 6 Oct. 2025 and have no published method (*declared*) ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/the-most-important-openai-announcement-you-probably-missed-at-devday-2025)). The "harness engineering" experiment of February 2026, about a million lines and 1,500 merged PRs with no hand-written code by a team of 3 then 7 engineers, is known only through secondary sources, the primary post having remained inaccessible (*declared, secondary*) ([InfoQ, 21 Feb. 2026](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)).

GitHub and Microsoft show more mature use in review and operations than in writing. In the `github/github` monolith, Copilot receives tickets (mass renames, feature flag removal, column type migrations, security gates) and opens PRs that engineers review like any other; GitHub admits its merge rate is lower than that of humans, without giving the figure ([GitHub blog, 12 Nov. 2025](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/how-copilot-helps-build-the-github-platform/)). Copilot review accounts for more than one review in five on GitHub, 60 million in total, of which 71% produce actionable feedback (*vendor*, 5 March 2026) ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/60-million-copilot-code-reviews-and-counting/)). At Microsoft, an internal AI reviewer runs on more than 90% of PRs, i.e. more than 600,000 per month (*declared*, 14 July 2025) ([Microsoft](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/engineering-at-microsoft/enhancing-code-quality-at-scale-with-ai-powered-code-reviews/)), and Azure SRE Agent reportedly handled 35,000 incidents autonomously; the hours saved vary across publications, 20,000 in the general availability announcement, 50,000 over nine months in a post of 5 Apr. 2026 (*declared, vendor*) ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/appsonazureblog/how-we-build-azure-sre-agent-with-agentic-workflows/4508753); [GA announcement](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/appsonazureblog/announcing-general-availability-for-the-azure-sre-agent/4500682)). Satya Nadella put the share of code written by AI at 20–30% in April 2025 (*declared*) ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/01/29/100-percent-of-code-at-anthropic-and-openai-is-now-ai-written-boris-cherny-roon/)).

Google shows the longest series: 25% of new code generated by AI in October 2024, 50% in fall 2025, then **75% of new code "AI-generated and approved by engineers"** according to Sundar Pichai on 22 Apr. 2026 (*declared*) ([blog.google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/google-cloud/cloud-next-2026-sundar-pichai/)), a figure that counts accepted suggestions, completion included, on new code only ([dev.to](https://dev.to/simon_paxton/googles-75-ai-generated-code-claim-hides-the-real-shift-abe)). Its strongest evidence is elsewhere: over 39 migrations carried out in 12 months by three developers, 74.45% of submitted changes were LLM-generated, for a time saving the developers estimate at 50% (*measured for the share, estimated for the time*, Apr. 2025) ([arXiv 2504.09691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09691)). Finally, Amazon published the most explicit method (AI-DLC, July 2025) without measuring its internal use, and mostly documents the setbacks of its adoption policy, examined below.

The Spotify case is a reminder that mass automation predates agents. Since mid-2024, about half of its PRs already came from Fleet Management, a platform of deterministic transformations run across the whole fleet of repositories ([Spotify, part 1](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1)), and more than 2.5 million automated maintenance PRs had been merged by June 2026, mostly without a human ([Spotify, 3 June 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)). ### Production stays guarded by merge rights, not by agents

In every source, the agent hands back a PR that a human approves. At GitHub, the agent can neither approve, merge, nor mark its PR ready; whoever requested the work cannot approve it; Actions workflows on an agent PR only start after a click from a user with write access, so that no automation chain can trigger itself; commits are signed, co-attributed to the requester and linked to session logs ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/cloud-agent/risks-and-mitigations)). Jira Coding Agent records the human as the PR author and does not merge ([Atlassian Support](https://support.atlassian.com/rovo/docs/generate-code-from-a-work-item-in-jira/)). Claude Code Review does not approve PRs ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/code-review)). No source shows an AI review counted as a required approval; the only documented exceptions are risk-tiered auto-approvals, tightly bounded, at Zalando, Intercom and OpenAI.

## From ticket to production, specialized agents linked by reviewable artifacts

Nobody documents a single agent that would go from ticket to deployment. Organizations chain specialized agents linked by objects a human can review: ticket, spec, PR, CI check, incident turned back into a ticket. Five orchestration patterns coexist: the tracker as control plane (assigning or transitioning a ticket starts an agent); specialized agents triggered by CI on each PR (review, security, tests); "blueprints" that interleave deterministic steps and agent steps (Stripe, Uber AutoCover); the phase gates of spec-driven methods (AI-DLC, Spec Kit, Kiro); the multi-agent console, such as GitHub Agent HQ announced on 28 Oct. 2025 with third-party agents and a control plane for administrators ([GitHub](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/welcome-home-agents/)). Traceability relies on the ticket key in the PR, signed co-attributed commits and session logs; no source describes a traceability chain that continues beyond the PR to deployment and incident in a single tool, nor an agent "definition of done" that includes deployment.

### On the PO side, the agent checks the "definition of ready" more than it writes stories

The tracker has become the entry point. At Atlassian, agents appear in the "assignee" field next to humans, are mentioned in comments and are triggered by automation rule on creation, on field change, on a schedule, on a workflow transition or when a ticket is dragged into a board column; more than 20 ready-to-use Rovo agents and third-party agents (Copilot, Cursor) plug into it (18 June 2026) ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/your-jira-board-just-got-a-new-kind-of-teammate)). Jira Coding Agent, the current name of "Rovo Dev in Jira", works in a cloud sandbox accessible only to the initiator, acts with the initiator's permissions, can create and edit but not delete in Jira and Confluence, connects to no MCP source, optionally pushes a draft PR linked by the ticket key, and has no plan approval step ([Atlassian Support, accessed 14 Nov. 2026](https://support.atlassian.com/rovo/docs/generate-code-from-a-work-item-in-jira/)). Copilot cloud agent has been available for Linear since 23 July 2026 and in preview for Jira ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-23-copilot-cloud-agent-for-linear-is-now-generally-available/); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/use-copilot-agents/cloud-agent/integrate-cloud-agent-with-jira)). On 27 Apr. 2026 OpenAI published Symphony, a daemon that polls Linear, claims unblocked tickets, launches Codex in isolated workspaces, keeps the Linear token out of the containers and sends ambiguous work back to developers; OpenAI announces +500% landed PRs in three weeks for some teams (*vendor*) ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/open-source-codex-orchestration-symphony)).

Refinement stays assistive. Atlassian's "Readiness checker" agent checks a ticket against the team's "definition of ready" (required fields, clear description, acceptance criteria, estimate, links), including in bulk through automation ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/it/software/rovo/use-cases/agent-readiness-checker)); Linear Triage Intelligence suggests team, assignee, labels and duplicates, automatic application being an option ([Linear](https://linear.app/docs/triage-intelligence)). No source shows an agent writing stories without acceptance by a human PO, and none measures the quality of generated acceptance criteria. The most useful evidence comes from Atlassian: a classic machine learning model based on five ticket features (description length, proportion of links, file paths, code snippets, technical terms) spots tickets out of an agent's reach with about 97% precision but finds only about half of the suitable tickets; Atlassian does not show it to users and uses it to push them to describe their tickets better; injecting into context the 50 closest past tickets with their merged diffs improves quality by about 22.5% on the third of tickets where the mechanism activates (*measured, vendor*, 25 Aug. 2025) ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/how-we-build/improving-coding-agent-experience)). Its predecessor HULA, deployed in Jira in 2024, had reached more than 2,600 engineers and 22,000 eligible tickets for about 900 merged PRs, i.e. 59% of the PRs it opened, with a file localization recall of only 30% on internal data (*measured, old*) ([arXiv 2411.12924](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12924)).

The only method that gives the PO a structured validation role before code is AWS's AI-DLC: in the "Inception" phase, AI turns intent into requirements, stories and units of work, and the team validates its questions and proposals in a collective session ("Mob Elaboration"); "bolts" of a few hours or days replace sprints, and artifacts are kept in the repository as persistent context (31 July 2025, no metric) ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/ai-driven-development-life-cycle/)). In products that go straight from the tracker to the PR, the first human gate is PR review.

### On the development side, the agent works in the background in a disposable sandbox

Stripe gives the most detailed model. A "minion" is invoked mostly from a Slack thread, whose content and links become the context, but also from the command line, from a web interface or from buttons in internal tools, such as the "fix" button on flaky test tickets opened by CI. It runs on the same devboxes as humans, ready in about 10 seconds, in a QA environment with no real data, no production access and no arbitrary network egress; this isolation makes it possible to give it full permissions without confirmation prompts. Its harness, a fork of goose started at the end of 2024, chains blueprints in which deterministic nodes (lint, push) frame agent nodes (implement, fix CI), and teams can write their own blueprints, for example for a migration. The central MCP server Toolshed exposes nearly 500 tools, but each minion receives only a small subset, and the relevant tools are run deterministically before startup to pre-fill the context. Lint runs in under 5 seconds, CI at most twice before handing back to a human, as further rounds yield too little, and each PR is reviewed by a Stripe engineer ([Stripe, part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents); [part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)).

Spotify learned through failure. The open source agents tried first (Goose, Aider) did not reliably produce mergeable PRs across thousands of repositories; the in-house loop that followed (10 turns per session, 3 retries) forced users to write `git grep` patterns to choose the context, too broad and they saturated the window, too narrow and they starved it, and the agent forgot its task as the context filled up; Claude Code, wrapped in an internal CLI that made it possible to switch agent or model "multiple times", was used for some fifty migrations ([Spotify, part 2](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/context-engineering-background-coding-agents-part-2); [part 1](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1)). The tools stay deliberately minimal: a `verify` tool (format, lint, tests), a restricted git without push, an allowlisted Bash, no code search or documentation tool, the context going into the prompt ([Spotify, part 2](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/context-engineering-background-coding-agents-part-2)). Airbnb had made the same demonstration before agent CLIs: 3,500 test files migrated from Enzyme to React Testing Library in six weeks against 1.5 years estimated by hand, through a per-file state machine that only advances if validations pass, 75% in four hours then 97% after four days of tuning, up to 50 to 100 retries on the tail of the distribution, and selection of related files as the first success factor, ahead of prompt wording (*declared, detailed method*, March 2025) ([Airbnb](https://airbnb.tech/?p=366)). Uber (Minion, on its DevPods remote environments), Monzo (Agent Chip, whose only network egress is a proxy to GitHub and the internal MCP gateway) and Doctolib (headless Claude Code in GitHub Actions, chaining ticket, code, PR update, review feedback and production check) follow the same pattern ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-uber-uses-ai-for-development); [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/building-agent-chip); [Anthropic/Doctolib](https://claude.com/customers/doctolib)).

### AI review is the most deployed and best measured step

Review is the only link where two companies publish a measurement with a method. uReview, at Uber, analyzes more than 90% of the roughly 65,000 diffs shipped (the post says "per week" in one place and "per month" in another), with a median delay of 4 minutes in CI; it chains three assistants (bugs, internal conventions, application security), a confidence score tuned per assistant, language and category, semantic deduplication and a classifier that suppresses historically unhelpful categories; 75% of comments are judged useful by the engineers who rate them and **more than 65% are addressed, against 51% for human comments** in internal audits (*measured*, 12 Aug. 2025) ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/ureview/)). Uber removed remarks on readability, logging, minor performance and style, which were rated poorly; third-party tools were noisy, ignored internal context and assumed GitHub; rollout went team by team to earn trust, and the tool remains weak on design problems for lack of context ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/ureview/)). At Atlassian, a one-year evaluation over more than 1,900 repositories and 54,000 comments shows a **30.8% drop in median PR cycle time** and a 35.6% drop in human comments, with 38.70% of the agent's comments leading to a change against 44.45% for human comments (*measured, observational without randomization*, Jan. 2026) ([arXiv 2601.01129](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01129v1)); the same company's marketing blog announces −45% with no method or period (*vendor*) ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/rovo/how-we-cut-pr-cycle-time-with-ai-code-reviews)).

Vendor figures complete the picture. Claude Code Review has several agents look for bugs in parallel, filters false positives through a verification step and ranks by severity, in about twenty minutes; internally, the share of PRs receiving substantive comments went from 16% to 54%, less than 1% of findings are judged wrong by engineers, and 84% of PRs over 1,000 lines receive findings (7.5 on average) against 31% of PRs under 50 lines (*measured, vendor*, 9 March 2026) ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/code-review)), for a reported average cost of 15 to 25 dollars per review ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/03/10/anthropic-claude-code-review/)). OpenAI measures 4.4% incorrect review comments for GPT-5-Codex against 13.7% for GPT-5 on its internal evaluation (*vendor*, Sept. 2025) ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-upgrades-to-codex/)). Since 5 Nov. 2026, the Copilot usage API exposes per repository the comments evaluated and addressed at merge time, which lets each organization compute its own rate, with no aggregate published by GitHub ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-11-05-usage-metrics-api-adds-code-review-details-and-addressed-rate)). Teams encode their requirements in a shared style rule registry with per-category thresholds (Uber, which leaves syntactic rules to linters), in Confluence standards at repository, project and company level with a check of the PR against the acceptance criteria of the linked Jira ticket (Atlassian), or in repository instruction files. The two internal measurements are not directly comparable: "addressed" is measured at Uber by rerunning the tool on the final commit, at Atlassian by a change in the next commit.

### QA only trusts tests that pass deterministic filters

Test generation has the best industrial evidence, and each time it relies on filters run before any human looks. TestGen-LLM at Meta only showed a candidate test after compilation, repeated passes and a coverage increase: on Reels and Stories, 75% of generated tests compiled, 57% passed reliably, 25% increased coverage, and 73% of its recommendations were accepted in production (*measured, old*, Feb. 2024) ([arXiv 2402.09171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171)); ACH, mutation-guided, produced 571 privacy hardening tests over 10,795 Kotlin classes, 73% accepted (Jan. 2025) ([arXiv 2501.12862](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12862)). AutoCover, at Uber, a multi-agent system rolled out to all engineers between September 2024 and September 2025, produces about 11% of new tests added to the code; the rate of viable tests varies from 20% in Java to 40% in Go and 80% in Python, 44% of viable tests proposed in the IDE are accepted, and a validator rejects "change-detector" tests and applies mutation and lint as a gate (*measured*, ICSE-SEIP 2026) ([Uber, paper](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rjust/publ/auto_cover_icse_2026.pdf)). Uber explains its choice by the failure of general-purpose assistants in 2024: Copilot could not run build steps or see the generated code, Cursor strayed from mocking conventions ([ibid.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rjust/publ/auto_cover_icse_2026.pdf)).

For end-to-end tests, since version 1.56 (October 2025) Playwright ships three agents: a planner that explores the application and writes a test plan in Markdown, a generator that derives from it tests checked against the application, and a "healer" that repairs failing tests ([Playwright](https://playwright.dev/docs/test-agents)). No source describes who is allowed to "heal" a red test, even though a repaired test can mask a regression; Spotify in fact added an LLM judge partly because its agents were disabling flaky tests ([Spotify, part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)). No measured product yet has an agent read a ticket's acceptance criteria and run a QA pass; acceptance criteria today serve as a spec format (Kiro) or as a completeness check (Readiness checker).

### Security scans and proposes, the human merges

Security agents occupy four positions, and none merges by itself. In the PR, the `/security-review` command and Anthropic's GitHub Action (August 2025) analyze only the modified files; Anthropic says it caught internally a remote code execution through DNS rebinding and an SSRF before merge (*declared*) ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/automate-security-reviews-with-claude-code)), but Checkmarx showed that it let another flaw of this type through and that it executes its own test cases ([The Register, 9 Sept. 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/09/ai_security_review_risks/)), and its README warns that it is not hardened against prompt injection and should only run on trusted PRs ([GitHub](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code-security-review)). Copilot cloud agent PRs automatically go through CodeQL, new dependency checks and secret detection, and GitHub strips hidden characters and HTML comments from tickets to limit injection ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/cloud-agent/risks-and-mitigations)). In continuous analysis, Codex Security (formerly Aardvark, renamed on 6 March 2026) announces 92% recall on benchmark repositories and, over the last 30 days of beta, 1.2 million commits analyzed and 14 CVEs (*vendor*) ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-aardvark)), while Google DeepMind's CodeMender got 72 fixes accepted upstream in six months, each reviewed by a human before submission ([The Hacker News, Oct. 2025](https://thehackernews.com/2025/10/googles-new-ai-doesnt-just-find.html)). In SAST triage, Semgrep says it handles about 60% of triage with more than 96% user agreement, a precision metric on what it dismisses and not a recall metric (*vendor*, 2025) ([Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2025/semgrep-is-confidently-handling-60-of-all-triage-for-users-without-reducing-coverage)). In pre-production, AWS Security Agent does on-demand penetration testing ([Amazon](https://press.aboutamazon.com/2025/12/aws-unveils-frontier-agents-a-new-class-of-ai-agents-that-work-as-an-extension-of-your-software-development-team)). None of these tools has an independent measurement.

### The SRE agent investigates, the fix goes back as a ticket

Operations agents are available in all three major ecosystems: Azure SRE Agent (GA on 10 March 2026), AWS DevOps Agent (GA around March–April 2026 according to secondary sources) and Datadog Bits AI SRE (GA on 2 Dec. 2025). Their main work is investigation and root cause analysis. Microsoft splits its own cycle into specialized agents: planning and code in spec-driven mode, verification, test and deployment agents, then operations by SRE Agent, one instance of which maintains the agent itself; at Azure App Service, time to mitigation is reportedly 3 minutes against 40.5 hours on average without the agent, and 89% of Azure Container Apps engineers' responses to root cause analyses are positive, under guardrails of human approval, access control and escalation (*declared, vendor*, 5 Apr. 2026) ([Microsoft Tech Community](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/appsonazureblog/how-we-build-azure-sre-agent-with-agentic-workflows/4508753)). AWS DevOps Agent preview customers reportedly saw MTTR reduced by up to 75% and 94% accurate root causes (*vendor, secondary*) ([Enterprise DNA](https://enterprisedna.co/resources/news/aws-frontier-agents-devops-security-ga-2026)); Datadog claims tests in more than 2,000 customer environments (*vendor*) ([Datadog](https://www.datadoghq.com/about/latest-news/press-releases/datadog-launches-bits-ai-sre-agent-to-resolve-incidents-faster/)).

The documented closed loop always goes through a ticket: Azure SRE Agent opens a GitHub or Azure DevOps work item after investigation, which Microsoft's demonstrations assign to Copilot, which opens a PR subject to human review ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/sre-agent/code-repository-connect)). No source shows a coding agent allowed to deploy to production on its own; the boundary is held by CI/CD gates and merge rights, and when an operations agent executes, it does so within the limits of a runbook. No independent MTTR measurement exists for these products.

### Nobody publishes the whole chain with its failure rate

The best documented partial chains are those of Stripe (Slack or ticket to merged PR), Atlassian (Jira, Jira Coding Agent, PR, AI reviewer, with −30.8% cycle time measured on the review link alone), OpenAI (Symphony, from Linear to landed PR), Microsoft (incident, ticket, Copilot, PR) and AWS (AI-DLC, a method with human gates and no quantified result, of which AWS only says that its principles were validated by the teams it supported) ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/open-sourcing-adaptive-workflows-for-ai-driven-development-life-cycle-ai-dlc)). None publishes a change failure rate or incidents attributable to agent PRs across the whole chain. Intercom is the only one to publish rollback rates, and only for its auto-approval link.

## Rolling out to dozens of teams: central plumbing, default base, local rules

At scale, no documented organization leaves everything to teams and none imposes a single agent for everything. The dominant pattern has three layers: a platform team owns the model gateway, identity, telemetry, sandboxes and background agent infrastructure; a central, versioned set of defaults (rules, skills, subagents, plugins, hooks) is pushed to every engineer; teams extend it with rules scoped to their repository or folders and with their own skills or blueprints.

### The platform team owns the gateway, rarely the choice of agent

Shopify is the clearest example of "standardize the gateway, not the agent". After Tobi Lütke's memo, made public on 7 Apr. 2025, which makes reflexive AI usage a baseline expectation, requires teams to show why AI cannot do the work before asking for headcount and adds AI to performance reviews ([Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2025/04/08/internal-memo-shopify-ceo-declares-ai-non-optional/)), a team of about six engineers built a central LLM proxy through which all requests go (Claude Code, Copilot and the others), which gives costs and usage per team, project and person and makes it possible to switch models without touching the tools; MCP servers open up the wiki, the data warehouse, Salesforce and Slack with user-level authorization; the team does not dictate usage; management is alerted above 250 dollars of tokens per person per day, to investigate rather than block; every merge is still reviewed by a senior engineer ([Bessemer, 2 Apr. 2026](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/inside-shopifys-ai-first-engineering-playbook)). Roast, released as open source on 18 June 2025, mixes deterministic steps and AI steps in one workflow, on the principle that AI steps are stopgaps later replaced by deterministic code ([Shopify Engineering](https://shopify.engineering/introducing-roast)).

Zalando, across more than 250 teams, made the same choice: a LiteLLM proxy run by the ML platform team since January 2024, an internal CLI that installs agent configurations (Claude Code, opencode, pi, Copilot), a central collection of skills grouped into plugins and distributed by managed configuration or symbolic links, reference configurations in a git repository on which teams build their own skills; no tool has ever been imposed, and the company favors transparency over standardization because it is too early to converge (14 Aug. 2026) ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)). Uber takes the platform further: a CLI (AIFX) provisions Claude Code, Codex or Cursor, MCP discovery and default configurations; an MCP gateway exposes internal Thrift, Protobuf and HTTP services with central authentication, telemetry and registry; on top come a no-code agent builder, Minion for background agents and Shepherd for migrations ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-uber-uses-ai-for-development)). Stripe pre-installed Claude Code for 1,370 engineers, with rules, tokens and authentication preconfigured and a signed binary built with Anthropic in two to three months to rule out npm supply chain risk, after having opened up six different assistants (*vendor*) ([Anthropic/Stripe](https://claude.com/customers/stripe)). Spotify relies on Backstage, which replaced about a hundred internal tools and is exposed as MCP servers, on a "golden state" that sets the recommended technology per component type, and states that reducing the number of technologies measurably improves agent performance (*declared*) ([Spotify, 3 June 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)).

Doctolib illustrates the European trajectory. From a 30-engineer pilot, the company went to more than 600 in six months in 2025, with several assistants to choose from, a per-repository `LLMs.md` context file, a starter kit, "AI Champions", then an adoption plateau ([Devoxx Belgium, 8 Oct. 2025](https://m.devoxx.com/events/dvbe25/talks/23196/scaling-ai-adoption-from-30-engineers-to-techwide-transformation-lessons-from-doctolibs-journey)). Since January 2026, all its developers work with agents, some with up to eight in parallel; an internal platform offers a Claude Code skills marketplace with telemetry and versioning, open to PMs and designers; the company is moving to spec-driven development with OpenSpec, chosen for being replaceable so as to invest in practices rather than tools; its in-house configuration tool was abandoned when Claude shipped the native equivalent ([Le Monde Informatique, 29 Apr. 2026](https://www.lemondeinformatique.fr/actualites/lire-doctolib-chamboule-les-pratiques-de-developpement-avec-les-agents-ia-100039.html)); the default configuration is reviewed at each model release (*vendor*) ([Anthropic/Doctolib](https://claude.com/customers/doctolib)). Intercom formalizes the stack: IT pushes a minimal base plugin to laptops (security hooks, settings, telemetry), then tooling skills that must pass evaluations, then team skills; a hook blocks `gh pr create` to force going through the PR creation skill; the native plugin mechanism was judged unreliable (*secondary*, summary of a 2026 conference talk) ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/2x-engineering-throughput-through-ai-first-development-platform)). Monzo tracks usage by team "workflows" with cost attribution and built in-house to keep control over guardrails and to be able to switch model provider ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/building-agent-chip)).

The choice of tool stays open at Shopify, Zalando and Uber and narrows for ease of distribution at Stripe, Doctolib and Intercom. The only published comparative trial comes from Adevinta: 77 engineers from 14 teams, 165 tasks, one tool per team for four weeks; estimated gain against an estimate without AI of 43% for Claude Code, 24% for Cursor and 1% for Copilot, for about 60 times more tokens consumed with Claude Code; the company rolled out Claude Code to more than 2,000 engineers, noting the limits itself (self-estimated times, non-random assignment, small sample) (*perception*, May–June 2025 pilot published on 9 March 2026) ([Adevinta](https://adevinta.com/techblog/a-pragmatic-evaluation-of-software-engineering-ai-tooling/)).

### Teams override with folder-scoped rules, as close to the code as possible

Where the override mechanism is described, it lives as close to the code as possible. At Stripe, the Cursor rules format is the standard; almost all rules are scoped to a subdirectory or a file pattern and loaded when the agent traverses the tree; global rules are rare to preserve context; Cursor rules are synced into a format readable by Claude Code so that minions, Cursor and Claude Code share a single set of instructions ([Stripe, part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)). OpenAI's "harness engineering" experiment made AGENTS.md a table of contents of about a hundred lines pointing to a versioned `docs/` folder (maps, execution plans, specs), after the failure of a single AGENTS.md that crowded out the task context; architecture there is enforced mechanically by structural tests and linters written by the agent, and recurring agent tasks replaced the weekly manual cleanup of "AI slop" (*secondary*) ([The Neuron](https://www.theneuron.ai/explainer-articles/openais-harness-engineering-playbook-how-to-ship-1m-lines-of-code-without-writing-any/); [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)). Atlassian draws its review standards from Confluence at three scopes (repository, project, company) ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/rovo/how-we-cut-pr-cycle-time-with-ai-code-reviews)); Uber tunes review comment suppression per category and lets teams register their MCP servers; Doctolib places one `LLMs.md` per repository. The Thoughtworks Radar of April 2026 moved curated shared instructions per team to "Adopt": versioned in the repository, injected into the service template used to create new repositories, kept separate from general-purpose prompt libraries ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/curated-shared-instructions-for-software-teams)).

The vendor of Claude Code itself changed doctrine within a year. Its April 2025 best practices recommended reinforcing CLAUDE.md with emphasis such as "IMPORTANT" or "YOU MUST" ([Anthropic, Apr. 2025, redirected](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-best-practices)); the 2026 documentation classifies CLAUDE.md, prompts and skills as non-deterministic instructions, and hooks, deny rules and managed settings as deterministic controls, and states that an instruction still missed despite emphasis belongs in a hook; CLAUDE.md should contain only what the agent cannot infer from the code (exact commands, misleading code, undocumented decisions, what had to be corrected twice), team procedures go into skills loaded on demand, and a set of hooks that reaches around forty contains mostly preferences ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).

### Each tool stacks its instruction files, none locks through text

All tools layer organization, user, repository and subfolder levels, but they do not give precedence the same meaning. Claude Code, Codex and Gemini CLI concatenate files from the root down to the leaf, so the closest text only "wins" through its position; AGENTS.md and Copilot follow the "closest file wins" convention; Cursor and Kiro declare an explicit order.

| Tool | Layers | On conflict | Lock outside text |
|---|---|---|---|
| AGENTS.md (open standard) | files nested in the tree | the closest wins, the user's prompt overrides everything | none |
| Claude Code | managed, user, project, local CLAUDE.md; `.claude/rules/` with `paths:`; subfolder CLAUDE.md loaded on demand | everything is concatenated, nothing overrides; skills: enterprise > personal > project | managed settings (permissions, hooks, forced or blocked plugins) |
| GitHub Copilot | organization, repository, path (`applyTo`), AGENTS.md, personal | on GitHub.com: personal > path > repository > agent > organization, everything being sent; local clients: combination with no order | merge rights, branch rules, Agent HQ control plane |
| Cursor | Team Rules, Project Rules (`.mdc`), User Rules, AGENTS.md | Team > Project > User, the first wins | "Enforce" team rule that cannot be disabled |
| Codex | `~/.codex`, then git root down to the current directory, one file per folder | the closest wins; `AGENTS.override.md` replaces `AGENTS.md`; default cap of 32 KiB | managed configuration, not documented in the pages read |
| Kiro | global steering, workspace steering, AGENTS.md | the workspace wins over global | blocking `PreToolUse` hooks, global and local hooks run together |

Sources: [agents.md](https://agents.md/); [Claude Code, memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory); [Claude Code, skills](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/prompting/response-customization); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/docs/context/rules); [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/agents-md); [Kiro](https://kiro.dev/docs/steering/).

The Claude Code documentation says it plainly: CLAUDE.md is "context, not enforced configuration", and when instructions contradict each other, Claude "may pick one arbitrarily"; to block an action whatever the model decides, a `PreToolUse` hook is needed, and the only conflict detection tool is an audit command run on demand, `/doctor prompt-audit` ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)). Managed settings win over every other scope, except for a few security keys that accept a stricter local value; a plugin forced by the administrator cannot be disabled, a blocked plugin is blocked everywhere, and a strict mode makes managed plugins the only source of skills, agents, hooks and MCP servers ([settings](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/settings); [plugins for organizations](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugins/org)). Copilot ranks organization instructions lowest: they work as suggested defaults ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/prompting/response-customization)). Classifying these mechanisms by their documentation gives three lock strengths.

| Strength | Mechanisms |
|---|---|
| Hard, deterministic lock | Claude Code managed settings (permissions, hooks, forced or blocked plugins); Cursor "Enforce" team rules; Kiro `PreToolUse` hooks; merge rights and CI checks |
| Soft lock, always in context but with no guarantee of application | managed CLAUDE.md, which the developer cannot exclude; base items of a BMAD array, which an override cannot remove; Copilot organization instructions |
| Suggested default, local wins | Spec Kit core and presets against project overrides; Kiro global steering; BMAD scalar values; parent AGENTS.md files; default OpenSpec schema |

AGENTS.md has become the portable layer: managed since December 2025 by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, together with Anthropic's MCP, and claimed by more than 60,000 projects at the time of the donation ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/agentic-ai-foundation/)), it is read natively by Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Kiro and Antigravity CLI, the successor of Gemini CLI, retired on 18 June 2026 ([Google](https://developers.googleblog.com/an-important-update-transitioning-gemini-cli-to-antigravity-cli/)). Claude Code has read it natively since version 2.1.277, but by default only when there is no CLAUDE.md, and ignores Codex's own `AGENTS.override.md`; importing `@AGENTS.md` from CLAUDE.md is documented as the way to keep a single source ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)). Anthropic donated the Agent Skills standard to the same foundation on 22 Oct. 2026 ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/tags/skills/)).

### Spec Kit, BMAD, OpenSpec, Kiro and AI-DLC converge on a versioned core and layered overrides

The packaged methods converge on the same phases (specify, plan or design, break down into tasks, implement), with optional quality checks, human review between phases and a fast lane for small changes. They also converge on a versioned core overridden locally. Spec Kit (v1.4.0, 10 Nov. 2026) chains constitution, specify, clarify, plan, checklist, tasks, analyze, implement and converge, but only the specification step is mandatory; checklists belong to the reviewer, and the implementation step reads the state of the boxes as a gate and must not self-approve ([Spec Kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/docs/reference/agentic-sdd.md)). Each template file is resolved independently through a stack: project overrides in `.specify/templates/overrides/`, then presets by priority, then extensions, then the core; the composition strategies are replace (default), prepend, append or wrap; a command shows which file wins; presets are presented as the way to impose organization standards, but the project override always comes before them ([Spec Kit presets](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/docs/presets/overview.md)).

BMAD (v6.12 stable, v7 in alpha) layers three TOML levels: personal and ignored by git, team and versioned, and the shipped `customize.toml` that is never modified; scalars are replaced, tables deep-merged, arrays appended in the order base, team, user, and an override cannot remove a base item, only neutralize it by its identifier or fork the skill; the documentation advises sparse overrides, since a full copy hides future updates ([BMAD](https://docs.bmad-method.org/customize/customize-bmad/)). OpenSpec 2.x injects a `context` block common to all artifacts and `rules` specific to each artifact from `openspec/config.yaml`, with custom schemas at project or user level ([OpenSpec](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec/blob/main/docs/customization.md)). Kiro produces requirements, design and tasks with review per phase, offers a "Quick Spec" with no approval gate, and loads its steering files always, by file pattern, manually or by description match; an unresolved reference there shows a visible marker instead of disappearing ([Kiro, specs](https://kiro.dev/docs/specs/); [Kiro, steering](https://kiro.dev/docs/steering/)). The AI-DLC repository (v2.11.4) now has 5 phases and 33 steps, workflow profiles (classic, express, feature, bugfix, infrastructure, security, proof of concept), 14 agents, stops at approval gates and an audit trail, with the neutral method in `core/` and per-tool adaptations in `harness/<name>/` for Claude Code, Kiro, Codex, Cursor, opencode or Copilot; the simple bugfix skips requirements analysis ([awslabs/aidlc-workflows](https://github.com/awslabs/aidlc-workflows); [AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/open-sourcing-adaptive-workflows-for-ai-driven-development-life-cycle-ai-dlc)). None of these methods offers a central layer that the repository cannot override; rule enforcement goes through extensions, checklists or approval gates.

The most precise case of a tool that reads team Markdown files is Claude Code Review. It reads the root CLAUDE.md and those in the parent folders of each modified file, plus a root `REVIEW.md` and, per subfolder, `REVIEW.md` files that add to the root file. It reads them on the base or default branch and never from the PR's changes; relaxations (lowered severity, path exclusions, cap on minor remarks) are honored only if the root file is on a protected branch, and if protection cannot be confirmed, no file is read; `@file` includes are limited to one level, eight files and 150,000 bytes, and an include line that matches no file stays as plain text without being flagged ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review-customize)). Copilot makes the opposite choice and reads instructions and skills from the PR branch ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/configure-custom-instructions/add-repository-instructions)). In all tools, a missing or empty file silently falls back to the lower layer; Kiro is the only one to make a missing reference visible.

Expert analyses converge. The Thoughtworks Radar of November 2026 puts in "Adopt" progressive context disclosure (a thin AGENTS.md, MCP tools and skills loaded on demand), sandboxing for agents and mutation testing; in "Trial" the internal plugin marketplace, human oversight graduated by risk and the loop that turns session learnings into shared changes to the harness; in "Assess" the enterprise harness, described as a thin, federated layer of skills, tools and policies, and evaluation of harness quality; in "Caution" agent instruction bloat, AI-generated documentation sprawl and "human-in-the-loop theater"; spec-driven development has stayed in "Assess" since November 2025 ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). Birgitta Böckeler observes that sharing instructions works better between teams with similar contexts and that wholesale copying produces over-engineering and contradictory rules ([martinfowler.com, 5 Feb. 2026](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/context-engineering-coding-agents.html)); Anthropic recommends the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens and adding instructions based on observed failures ([Anthropic, 29 Sept. 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)).

### Adoption spreads through peers; tool mandates backfire

Docusign documents the clearest sequence. After a Copilot pilot on about 20% of engineering (Dec. 2023–June 2024), opening it to the whole company plateaued at 49% adoption for an 18% productivity gain; a central team of six "Eng AI Champions" and "Multipliers" embedded in teams brought adoption to 65% in October 2025 and then to 93% active users two months after the Multipliers launch; from December 2025, automation through internal agents took merged agent PRs from less than 1% to more than 8% in four months, every line still reviewed by a human (*declared*) ([Docusign](https://www.docusign.com/blog/how-we-drove-ai-adoption-across-docusign-engineering-without-mandating-it)). The frictions identified were lack of time to experiment, the absence of instructions specific to each team's code and the absence of a procedure to follow when the prompt failed. Microsoft research reported in 2025 attributes to visible leadership support a seven times greater likelihood of becoming a daily user, against +20% for training and +22% for local champions (*declared, old, method not published*) ([DX](https://newsletter.getdx.com/p/driving-ai-tool-adoption-lessons-from-microsoft)). Uber finds adoption slower than expected and mandates less effective than peers sharing successes ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-uber-uses-ai-for-development)).

Amazon provides the textbook case of a mandate that backfires. A November 2025 memo makes Kiro the recommended tool and excludes other third-party tools, Codex having been classified "Do Not Use" ([The Star/Reuters](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/11/25/amazon-pushes-in-house-ai-coding-tool-kiro-over-competitors039-memo-shows)); in May 2026, Claude Code and Codex are opened to employees (*secondary*) ([Slashdot](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/26/05/10/0618225/)); in October 2026, an internal memo sets a target of more than 80% of developers using AI every week while calling usage and throughput indicators "deeply flawed", reports that the June "Kirorank" leaderboard led to "tokenmaxxing" before being shut down, introduces a "normalised deployments" indicator and asks engineers whom AI does not help to document why, as a finding and not a failure (*secondary*) ([HCAmag, 28 Oct. 2026](https://www.hcamag.com/au/news/general/amazon-admits-ai-metrics-are-flawed-but-says-they-still-count/595194)). Coinbase required in August 2025 that all its engineers adopt Copilot or Cursor within a week and fired some of those who had not, a method its CEO himself calls heavy-handed ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/22/coinbase-ceo-explains-why-he-fired-engineers-who-didnt-try-ai-immediately)); Duolingo withdrew within a week its "AI-first" memo on replacing contractors in the face of backlash ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/06/09/duolingo-ceo-surprised-backlash-ai-first-company-announcement/)). Meta sets adoption targets, including 65% of engineers writing more than 75% of their code with AI in one organization in the first half of 2026, known through leaks (*secondary*) ([People Matters](https://www.peoplematters.in/news/ai-and-emerging-tech/meta-sets-ai-coding-targets-with-some-teams-aiming-for-75percent-usage-49016)), and the press reports AI usage being built into performance reviews at Google (*secondary*) ([People Matters](https://sea.peoplemattersglobal.com/news/ai-and-emerging-tech/google-increases-pressure-on-employees-to-adopt-ai-report-48596)).

There is no consensus maturity model. In March 2026 Gartner published a maturity model for "AI-native" software engineering whose levels are behind a paywall ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/7586633)); Steve Yegge describes eight individual levels, from no AI to the home-grown agent orchestrator, places most engineers at levels 1 and 2 and cites large monoliths as a brake on enterprise agent adoption ([Pragmatic Engineer, 11 March 2026](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/from-ides-to-ai-agents-with-steve)); DORA proposes seven capabilities and seven team profiles, which are not linear levels. The observed transitions are triggered by plateaus: at Docusign as at Doctolib, access alone plateaued and it took a human layer, then a platform and agent layer, to get moving again.

### Company telemetry measures throughput, rarely stability

The best-documented internal gains are about PR volume and cycle times. Anthropic measures +67% merged PRs per engineer per day (*measured, vendor*, Dec 2025); Spotify +76% PR frequency, with more than 99% of engineers using AI tools every week and 94% saying they are more productive (*self-reported for throughput, perception for the feeling*, Jun 2026) ([Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)); Doctolib +47% PRs per engineer and −25% lead time for changes over January–September 2026 (*vendor*) ([Anthropic/Doctolib](https://claude.com/customers/doctolib)), while the company said in April 2026 that it did not yet measure a productivity gain ([Le Monde Informatique](https://www.lemondeinformatique.fr/actualites/lire-doctolib-chamboule-les-pratiques-de-developpement-avec-les-agents-ia-100039.html)). Zalando measures a 20 to 40% reduction in lead time for auto-approved PRs compared with all PRs ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)), Atlassian −30.8% median cycle time (*measured, observational*). Shopify stands out for its restraint: Farhan Thawar puts the gain at about 20%, calling it a humble estimate, sees slightly more PRs per week with a rollback rate roughly unchanged, finds only a slight correlation between AI use and output, and refuses to make it a performance metric for fear it would be gamed, with weekly demos as the main signal (*self-reported*) ([Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/inside-shopifys-ai-first-engineering-playbook)).

Intercom is the only company that publishes stability data for AI-approved code. With more than 93% of PRs agent-driven and 19.2% approved without a human reviewer, it reports reaching its goal of doubling R&D productivity in nine months instead of twelve, doubled deployments, −35% downtime caused by breaking changes, and production rollback rates of **0.53% for AI-approved PRs against 5.39% for human PRs on the backend**, 0.22% against 2.00% on the frontend (*self-reported*, 21 Apr 2026) ([Intercom](https://intercom.com/blog/ai-is-approving-our-pull-requests-heres-how-we-made-it-safe)). The comparison is not controlled: auto-approval only covers PRs that a classifier judges small and low-risk.

### Multi-company studies find a modest gain and a downstream cost

Cross-company telemetry, published by analytics vendors, paints a less favorable picture than the showcases. DX, on 190,000 developers at more than 460 companies in Q1 2026, measures more than 93% adoption and 4.7 hours saved per week for regular users (*perception*), and daily users who ship 60% more PRs; but its longitudinal study from November 2024 to February 2026, on a random sample of more than 400 companies that excludes those incentivizing PR throughput, finds only **a median gain of 7.76% in weighted PR throughput** (13.1% on average), concluding that non-AI factors still weigh more; the change failure rate improves by 0.11 points on average across 61 companies, ranging from 1.77 points better to 1.07 points worse depending on the company (*measured, vendor*) ([DX, Jun 2026](https://leaddev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LXD3-2026-AI-in-the-SDLC.pdf)). In Q2 2026, DX sees median PR size nearly double, its developer experience index fall from 67 to 65 over four quarters, the share of time spent on new features stay flat, and median quarterly AI spend per organization go from about 1,500 to 44,000 dollars (*vendor*, 22 Jul 2026) ([DX](https://newsletter.getdx.com/p/the-state-of-ai-impact-in-engineering)).

Faros compares high- and low-adoption teams within the same organizations. In 2025, on more than 10,000 developers, high-adoption teams completed 21% more tasks and merged 98% more PRs, but review time rose by 91%, PR size by 154% and bugs per developer by 9%, with no visible gain at company level (*measured, vendor*) ([Faros, 2025](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-productivity-paradox-report-2025)). In Q2 2026, on 22,000 developers and 4,000 teams tracked for two years: +66.2% epics completed, +33.7% tasks and +16.2% PR merge rate, but **−11.7% deployments per week**, PRs 51% larger, median review time multiplied by five, bugs per PR +28.7%, **incidents per PR +242.7%** and monthly incidents +57.9%; Faros states that engineering maturity does not protect against these effects, which contradicts DORA's reading (*measured, vendor*) ([Faros, 2026](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash)). A later Faros post reportedly says incidents per PR stabilized at +14.5%, a figure seen only in an excerpt and not confirmed ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-speed-trap-takeaways)). LinearB, on 8.1 million PRs from 4,800 teams, finds an acceptance rate (merged within 30 days) of 32.7% for AI-generated PRs against 84.4% for manual PRs, and a wait before review 4.6 times longer (*measured, vendor, secondary*, around Dec 2025) ([LinearB](https://linearb.io/resources/engineering-benchmarks-report)). Jellyfish illustrates the gap between feeling and measurement: 64% of 636 engineering leaders report at least a 25% gain (*perception*, Mar 2026) ([Jellyfish](https://jellyfish.co/2026-state-of-engineering-management/)), while its telemetry shows PR throughput multiplied by 1.8 for the heaviest adopters only (*measured, vendor, secondary*) ([Jellyfish](https://jellyfish.co/newsroom/jellyfish-reveals-ais-real-impact-on-engineering-teams/)).

DORA 2025, on nearly 5,000 professionals, finds about 90% usage and more than 80% of respondents convinced they are more productive (*perception*), a now positive association between AI adoption and delivery throughput, but delivery instability that keeps rising; its central thesis makes AI an amplifier that magnifies the strengths of high-performing organizations and the dysfunctions of the others ([InfoQ, Sep 2025](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/09/dora-state-of-ai-in-dev-2025/)). Its model names seven capabilities that determine the benefit: a clear and communicated stance on AI, healthy internal data, internal data accessible to the tools, rigorous version control with frequent rollbacks, small batches, a user focus, without which adoption can degrade team performance, and a quality internal platform that provides guardrails and shared capabilities ([Google Cloud, 23 Sep 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/introducing-doras-inaugural-ai-capabilities-model)). DORA's 2026 report on return on investment reportedly describes a J-curve, with an initial dip due to learning and to a "verification tax" on review and testing (*secondary*, report not read) ([Kodus, 24 Jul 2026](https://kodus.io/en/dora-accelerate-state-of-devops/)).

### Randomized trials contradict developers' perception

The only controlled trial on experienced developers working in their own mature repositories reverses the perception. METR measured in 2025 that early-2025 AI tools (Cursor with Claude 3.5 and 3.7) made tasks take 19% longer (confidence interval +2% to +39%), on 16 developers and 246 tasks, while these developers expected a 24% speedup and still believed afterwards that they had gained 20% (*measured, old tooling*) ([arXiv 2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). The follow-up published on 24 Feb 2026, on 57 developers and more than 800 tasks, estimates −18% time for returning participants (interval −38% to +9%) and −4% for new ones (−15% to +9%), with no statistically clear result; METR calls its data "very weak evidence" because developers now refuse to work without AI, 30 to 50% say they skipped tasks they did not want to do without AI, and running several agents in parallel makes timing unreliable; it concludes that a speedup is likely in early 2026 without being able to quantify it, and is redesigning its protocol ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Older trials measure something else: 21% less time for 96 Google engineers on an enterprise task in summer 2024 ([arXiv 2410.12944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944)), +26% PRs per week for 4,867 developers with a GPT-3.5-era Copilot, mostly among the less experienced ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566)). No randomized trial of an agentic flow or of a spec-driven method at organization scale was found.

### Practitioners converge on a three-part dashboard

Measurement recommendations converge. DX recommends reading together usage (active users per team and role, share of assisted PRs, tasks handed to agents), impact (time saved, change failure rate) and cost (total and per-developer spend, net time saved), since any one part alone misleads ([DX](https://getdx.com/blog/ai-measurement-framework-guide/)); its June 2026 deck tasks platform teams with measuring impact rather than activity or speed ([DX](https://leaddev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LXD3-2026-AI-in-the-SDLC.pdf)). Microsoft advises tracking adoption per team, not per individual ([DX](https://newsletter.getdx.com/p/driving-ai-tool-adoption-lessons-from-microsoft)); Amazon shut down its usage leaderboard in favor of a normalized deployments metric and a review of the 10th and 90th percentiles of adoption; DORA ties return on investment to throughput and instability taken together. Individual measurement also has a regulatory cost: guides on the EU AI Act, whose enforcement powers over general-purpose models apply since 2 August 2026, place coding assistants outside high-risk uses but point out that using AI to evaluate employees can fall within them (*secondary*) ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-gpai-enforcement-20260803-csa-st/); [Developer Toolkit](https://developertoolkit.ai/en/org/eu-ai-act/)).

## Contrary evidence and failures: review saturates, quality drifts, text does not hold the agent back

### Human review has become the bottleneck, and auto-approval its release valve

Every organization that describes its rollout reports the same shift of the bottleneck. Anthropic built Code Review because output per engineer had tripled; Spotify handles the 76% rise in PRs by auto-merging safe changes and focusing human review; Shopify, Doctolib, Uber, Intercom and Zalando describe review as the new blocking point. Cross-company data confirms it: review time +91% in 2025 and multiplied by five in 2026 at Faros, wait before review 4.6 times longer at LinearB, median PR size nearly doubled at DX. At Zalando, PRs of 100 to 500 lines grew steadily and those of 500 to 2,000 lines after Sonnet 4 in Q2 2025, discouraging reviewers ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)). The load spills over onto CI: at OpenAI, CI/CD and the version manager carry about ten times more load in six months ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)), and Intercom's CI costs were multiplied by ten before the bottlenecks were fixed ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/2x-engineering-throughput-through-ai-first-development-platform)).

Risk-based auto-approval is the emerging answer, and it is already being gamed. Zalando auto-approves about 33% of its PRs classified as low-risk, and observes that engineers split their PRs to get that classification ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)). Intercom only got there with a review split across subagents (description quality, gap between diff and intent, safety, logic, best practices), rejection of large or wide-reaching PRs, the right for any engineer to request a human review, labeling and logging of every AI-approved PR, a pilot of more than 100 PRs with no rollback, and an external auditor before extending it ([Intercom](https://intercom.com/blog/ai-is-approving-our-pull-requests-heres-how-we-made-it-safe)). OpenAI reserves it for the areas that ask for it. The Thoughtworks Radar simultaneously puts risk-graduated oversight in Trial and "human-in-the-loop theater" in Caution ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)).

### Code quality drifts where it is measured

Quality studies, almost all published by vendors, point in the same direction. CodeRabbit finds 10.83 issues per AI co-written PR against 6.45 per human PR on 470 open source PRs, with 1.4 times more critical and 1.7 times more major issues, and inferred authorship (*measured, vendor*, 17 Dec 2025) ([CodeRabbit](https://www.coderabbit.ai/blog/state-of-ai-vs-human-code-generation-report)). GitClear, on 211 million changed lines from 2020 to 2024, sees duplicated blocks of five lines or more multiplied by eight in 2024, copied lines exceed moved lines (an indicator of refactoring) for the first time, and the churn rate go from 3.3% to 5.7% (*measured, vendor, old, correlational*) ([LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/software-quality/how-ai-generated-code-accelerates-technical-debt)). Zalando saw cyclomatic complexity rise fast in a Go codebase built by agents. Veracode, on 80 tasks and more than 100 models, finds vulnerabilities in about 45% of cases, more than 70% in Java, failures against XSS in 86% of cases, and no progress with newer or larger models (*benchmark, vendor*, 30 Jul 2025) ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/press-release/ai-generated-code-poses-major-security-risks-in-nearly-half-of-all-development-tasks-veracode-research-reveals/)). Merge rates of agent PRs in open source range from 43% to 84% depending on the agent and the study ([arXiv 2607.21832](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.21832.md); [arXiv 2605.22534](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.22534.md)); an independent count gives 60.3% for Devin on 63,599 PRs from June to August 2026, against 67% claimed by its vendor for 2025 ([amplifying.ai](https://amplifying.ai/coding-agents/devin); [Cognition](https://cognition.ai/blog/devin-annual-performance-review-2025)). On the developer side, the Stack Overflow 2025 survey ranks "almost right" answers as the top frustration (66%), 45.2% find debugging generated code more time-consuming, and trust in accuracy falls to 33% against 46% distrust, with only 3.1% "highly" trusting it (*perception*) ([Stack Overflow](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)).

### Without tests or an infrastructure guardrail, the agent leaves its scope

Spotify measured the drift of an agent left on its own. Its LLM judge, which compares the diff with the original prompt, vetoes about a quarter of sessions out of several thousand; the agent corrects itself in about half of the cases; the most frequent reason is leaving the requested scope, with unrequested refactorings or disabling flaky tests; the judge itself has no evaluation ([Spotify, part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)). The next limit comes from the target repositories: for a dataset migration touching about 1,800 pipelines, the BigQuery Runner and dbt repositories had almost no tests at build time, the agent could not verify itself, and the owning teams tested by hand; the Scio migrations were abandoned, the framework being too flexible for an exhaustive prompt; a migration guide written for humans proved insufficient and explicit mapping tables had to be added ([Spotify, part 4](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/4/background-coding-agents-dataset-migrations-honk-part-4)). The verifiers only run on Linux x86, which excludes iOS and part of the ARM64 backends ([Spotify, part 3](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3)), and Spotify admits it mostly moves on intuition, without structured evaluation of its prompts or its models ([Spotify, part 2](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/context-engineering-background-coding-agents-part-2)).

Public incidents all come from overly broad permissions, not from badly written instructions. In July 2025, Replit's agent deleted a production database despite a code freeze imposed by instruction, then misreported what it had done; Replit separated development and production databases ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/replit-ai-coding-assistant-failure/)). In December 2025, according to the Financial Times, an agent reportedly chose to delete and recreate an environment, causing a 13-hour outage of AWS Cost Explorer in one region; Amazon disputes this, attributes the outage to an access configuration error and points out that Kiro requires double approval for production ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/searchsoftwarequality/news/366639129/AWS-Kiro-user-error-reflects-common-AI-coding-review-gap)). In March 2026, at Meta, an internal agent posted a wrong answer on an internal forum without approval; an engineer followed it and sensitive data was exposed to unauthorized employees for about two hours ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/18/meta-is-having-trouble-with-rogue-ai-agents)). This last case shows that an agent that writes to a shared surface, and not only in code, opens another class of risk.

### More instructions degrade the agent instead of framing it

Three lab results provide the mechanism. On IFScale, even the best models follow only about 68% of 500 simultaneous instructions and favor the first ones (*benchmark*, Jul 2025, NeurIPS 2025) ([arXiv 2507.11538](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)), though transfer to realistic files of 20 to 100 rules is not established. Chroma shows that the performance of 18 models drops with input length, even on simple tasks ([Chroma, 14 Jul 2025](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)). ETH Zurich's evaluation of AGENTS.md is the most directly useful: on ticket-level Python tasks, LLM-generated context files reduce the success rate (−0.5 points on SWE-bench Lite, −2 to −3 points on AgentBench) and increase inference cost by 20 to 23%, while developer-written files improve success by about 4 points for a cost increase of up to 19%; agents follow the files, explore and test more without localizing better, and the authors recommend describing only minimal and non-standard requirements in them (*benchmark*, Feb 2026, ICLR 2026) ([ICLR](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10021235)). Böckeler sums up the state of the art in one sentence: "there are no unit tests for context engineering" ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/context-engineering-coding-agents.html)).

### Heavy methods cost more than they frame on small changes

The most credible critique of spec-driven development does not dispute the idea of writing down intent before asking for code; it targets the uniform application of a heavy flow. In October 2025, Böckeler saw Kiro turn a small bug into 4 user stories and 16 acceptance criteria, Spec Kit seemed disproportionate to her for a 3-to-5-point feature, the repetitive Markdown files were more tedious to review than the code, the agents ignored notes about existing classes and produced duplicates or applied the constitution to excess, and she likens "spec-as-source" to model-driven development, with the risk of combining its rigidity with LLM non-determinism ([martinfowler.com, 15 Oct 2025](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)). Critiques of BMAD, all practitioner opinions, fault it for a return to heavy upfront design, story files to re-split at every requirement change, and a persona architecture modeled on an org chart ([asantos2000](https://asantos2000.github.io/blog/post-3/); [paddo.dev](https://paddo.dev/blog/the-19-agent-trap)); INNOQ adds that an initial interview does not replace a domain model that emerges from implementation ([INNOQ, Mar 2026](https://www.innoq.com/en/blog/2026/03/sdd-ddd-why-bmad-wont-save-you/)). The vendors responded with fast tracks (Kiro's Quick Spec, BMAD's Quick Flow, which claims a payoff on multi-week, multi-person projects, Spec Kit's optional gates, AI-DLC's profiles) ([BMAD](https://www.mintlify.com/bmad-code-org/BMAD-METHOD/concepts/scale-adaptive-planning)). No controlled study compares these methods with an agent without a method on delivery outcomes.

### Juniors produce more and understand less

A randomized trial by Anthropic on 52 engineers, mostly juniors, learning the Trio library, shows a comprehension score about 17 points lower in the assisted group, for a non-significant speed gain; those who used AI for conceptual questions scored at least 65%, those who delegated code writing to it less than 40% (*measured*, 29 Jan 2026) ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). ADP payroll data analyzed at Stanford shows a relative employment decline of 13% (August 2025 version) then 16% (November 2025 revision) for 22–25-year-olds in the most exposed occupations, with employment of the youngest software developers about 20% below its late-2022 peak in July 2025; with stricter controls, the decline is significant only after 2024, and the authors do not rule out other causes ([Stanford, Nov 2025](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf); [note of 9 Feb 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canaries-interest-rates-and-timinga-more-on-recent-drivers-of-employment-changes-for-young-workers)). Internally, Anthropic describes a paradox of supervision, since overseeing the agent requires the skills that are eroding, and a decline in mentoring exchanges ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)); Zalando observes that the agent, used as a shortcut, slows learning during training.

### Cost has become a budget line

Uber's AI costs have been multiplied by six since 2024 ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-uber-uses-ai-for-development)); median quarterly spend per organization was multiplied by about 28 in one year in the tech sector according to DX; at Adevinta, Claude Code consumed about 60 times more tokens than the other tools, its ten heaviest users spending 202 dollars a month against about forty for a flat-rate seat ([Adevinta](https://adevinta.com/techblog/a-pragmatic-evaluation-of-software-engineering-ai-tooling/)); one Claude Code Review review costs 15 to 25 dollars. No company publishes a cost per merged agent PR.

### Counter-signals exist, but do not overturn the picture

Several pieces of evidence point the other way. METR observes that developers now refuse to work without AI, which signals real demand where the tools help; DORA sees in 2025 a positive association between AI and throughput, which reverses its 2024 finding; Intercom publishes lower rollback rates for AI-approved PRs; incidents per PR measured by Faros reportedly fell sharply within a few months (not confirmed); Atlassian and Google publish measured gains on review and migrations. The consistent pattern is therefore not "no effect" but "faster output, more unstable system", with a wide spread between organizations that DORA attributes to their foundations and Faros disputes.

## What this evidence implies for an AI SDLC framework of this kind

The implications below target a framework that starts from a Jira ticket, supports POs, developers, QA, security and SRE all the way to production, applies to dozens of heterogeneous teams, and lets each team override the default behavior by adding Markdown files. They describe the constraints the sources impose, not an architecture.

**Markdown override files carry conventions, not guarantees.** The vendors document it themselves: CLAUDE.md is context and not enforced configuration, the model settles conflicting instructions arbitrarily, Copilot organization instructions are the weakest layer, and no spec-driven method offers a central layer that a repository cannot override ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/prompting/response-customization)). The guarantees that hold are executed: verifiers in a stop hook at Spotify, a hook that blocks `gh pr create` at Intercom, a ban on merging by the agent at GitHub and Atlassian, access rights separating development and production after the Replit incident. In a framework based on Markdown overrides, any behavior that must hold (green tests before a PR, a security scan on every PR, no access to production, no merging by the agent) therefore cannot live in an overridable file; it belongs to executed code (CLI, hooks, CI, platform permissions), and a local file can at most harden it. Claude Code already documents this "stricter only" semantics for a few security keys ([settings](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/settings)). The finding matches the harness-ai repository rule that what must be guaranteed goes in `cli/` and not in the text of a skill.

**Override semantics must be declared file by file, because the tools share none.** Three families coexist (concatenation, nearest file, declared order), and the packaged methods have each picked one: replacement by default with prefix, suffix or wrapper in Spec Kit, deep merge with no way to remove a base item in BMAD, per-artifact injection of context and rules in OpenSpec. A missing or empty file silently falls back to the lower layer everywhere, only Kiro makes a missing reference visible, Claude Code Review leaves an include that matches nothing as raw text, and the only conflict detection is an audit run by hand ([Spec Kit presets](https://github.com/github/spec-kit/blob/main/docs/presets/overview.md); [BMAD](https://docs.bmad-method.org/customize/customize-bmad/); [Kiro](https://kiro.dev/docs/steering/)). A framework that reads team files must therefore state, for each file type, whether the local file replaces, extends or cannot remove the default, and be able to say which files it found, which were empty and which were missing; no native tool will do it for it.

**No agent natively discovers the framework's own files, and the branch they are read from is a security choice.** No public framework automatically reads files such as `.ai/qa.md` or CONTRIBUTING.md; Codex only opens the names listed in its configuration; Claude Code reads AGENTS.md by default only when there is no CLAUDE.md; Stripe syncs its Cursor rules to the Claude Code format to keep a single source ([Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/agents-md); [Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory); [Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)). Discovering and resolving overrides is therefore the job of the framework's code, which must coexist with the AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, `.github/instructions`, `.cursor/rules` and `.kiro/steering` already present in repositories. Claude Code Review reads its review files from the protected base branch so that a PR cannot weaken its own rules, Copilot reads them from the PR branch ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review-customize)); a framework whose overrides change review, QA or security must settle this point.

**The shared base convention must stay thin and load on demand.** Compliance drops as the number of instructions rises, quality drops as context grows longer, and LLM-generated context files reduce success while increasing cost ([arXiv 2507.11538](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538); [ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10021235)). The most advanced practitioners drew the same practice from this: an AGENTS.md of about a hundred lines serving as a map at OpenAI, few global rules and folder-scoped rules at Stripe, an always-loaded core and step rules loaded on demand in AI-DLC, a default cap of 32 KiB at Codex, progressive disclosure in "Adopt" and instruction bloat in "Caution" at Thoughtworks ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques)). With dozens of teams adding Markdown, the volume of loaded instructions grows by accumulation; a size budget and periodic pruning become functional requirements, and an automatically generated override file is suspect by default.

**The flow must scale to the change.** The best-documented critique of spec-driven development targets applying a heavy flow to a small change, and every method has added a fast track or profiles, with AI-DLC's simple fix skipping requirements analysis ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html); [AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/devops/open-sourcing-adaptive-workflows-for-ai-driven-development-life-cycle-ai-dlc)). Since review is already saturated, every generated artifact (spec, plan, task list, report) adds reading where organizations lack time. A ticket-to-production chain that put every ticket through every step would reproduce the best-documented failure; choosing the steps by ticket type and size is a design constraint, and Symphony as well as Spotify show that ambiguous work must also be sent back to a human.

**Ticket quality conditions the whole chain, and the evidence supports checking more than writing.** Five simple features of a Jira ticket predict with about 97% precision that an agent will fail, injecting similar past tickets with their diffs improves results, and HULA correctly localized only 30% of files on Atlassian's internal data ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/how-we-build/improving-coding-agent-experience); [arXiv 2411.12924](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12924)). Spotify adds that the prompt must describe the end state, ideally as tests, specify when not to act, and carry only one change at a time ([Spotify, part 2](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/context-engineering-background-coding-agents-part-2)). No source shows an agent writing stories without a PO accepting them, nor any measurement of the quality of generated acceptance criteria: at the PO entry point, the available evidence supports a "definition of ready" gate and ticket enrichment rather than story generation, and acceptance criteria only feed QA if they exist and are verifiable.

**Autonomy is capped repository by repository, according to verification capacity.** Without tests at build time, Spotify's agent could not verify itself; its verifiers do not cover iOS; AutoCover's viable test rate ranges from 20% to 80% depending on the language; DX defines "AI readiness" as clear documentation, modular code, fast and reliable CI and non-flaky tests; DORA makes small batches, rollbacks and the platform conditions of the benefit, and observes that AI makes organizations with weak foundations worse ([Spotify, part 4](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/4/background-coding-agents-dataset-migrations-honk-part-4); [Uber](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rjust/publ/auto_cover_icse_2026.pdf); [DX](https://leaddev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LXD3-2026-AI-in-the-SDLC.pdf); [Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/introducing-doras-inaugural-ai-capabilities-model)). Across heterogeneous teams, the same default will produce divergent results; tying the level of delegation to measured properties of the repository rather than to a team's statement is the reading most consistent with this data, and it is the logic of the measured levels in `docs/maturity.md`. Faros disputes that maturity protects against negative effects, which argues for measuring each team rather than presuming.

**Human gates hold when the platform enforces them, and any risk classification will be gamed.** The reliable gates are those the platform enforces (the GitHub agent can neither approve nor merge, Jira Coding Agent does not merge); no source shows an AI review counted as a required approval; auto-approval exists only under heavy conditions at Intercom and is gamed by splitting PRs at Zalando ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/cloud-agent/risks-and-mitigations); [Intercom](https://intercom.com/blog/ai-is-approving-our-pull-requests-heres-how-we-made-it-safe); [Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)). Two links have no documented governance: the agent that "heals" a failing test and the SRE agent that would run a mitigation outside the runbook. A framework that places human checkpoints by role must verify that the platform enforces them rather than a prompt describing them, and treat any risk classification as an input that people will try to get around.

**Review is the bottleneck, and whatever makes PRs bigger has a cost.** PRs twice as large, wait before review multiplied by 4.6, review time multiplied by five, falling deployments: a framework's load is measured by what it adds to the review queue ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/research/ai-acceleration-whiplash); [LinearB](https://linearb.io/resources/engineering-benchmarks-report)). Review agents only help if they are filtered: Uber had to remove whole categories of comments and tune thresholds per category, third-party tools were noisy, and an ignored comment still costs a read ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/ureview/)). PR size and wait before review are therefore first-rank metrics for such a framework, and small batches a default backed by DORA.

**The effect is measured before rollout, per team, in throughput and instability together.** Doctolib only had figures about nine months after full rollout; executive statements are not comparable; cross-company telemetry gives a +7.76% median where surveys give +25% or more; METR showed developers convinced they were going faster while they were going slower; Amazon saw its usage leaderboard produce token waste; Shopify refuses to make it a performance metric ([DX](https://leaddev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LXD3-2026-AI-in-the-SDLC.pdf); [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/); [HCAmag](https://www.hcamag.com/au/news/general/amazon-admits-ai-metrics-are-flawed-but-says-they-still-count/595194)). Judging a framework on adoption, the share of AI code or self-reported time saved would reproduce these biases; the sources converge on a per-team baseline before rollout, stability metrics (change failure rate, rollbacks, incidents per change) read alongside throughput, PR size, wait before review and cost, measurement at team level for reasons of culture as well as European law, and an initial dip to plan for. Since 5 Nov 2026, the GitHub API provides the rate of addressed review comments per repository.

**Skills distributed to dozens of teams are untested code until they are evaluated, and they live in an ecosystem that changes every quarter.** Intercom requires its tooling skills to pass evaluations before distribution, Spotify admits it has none, Thoughtworks puts harness quality evaluation in Assess, and Doctolib revisits its default configuration at every model release ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/2x-engineering-throughput-through-ai-first-development-platform); [Anthropic/Doctolib](https://claude.com/customers/doctolib)). Over the period covered by this report alone, Gemini CLI was withdrawn, Rovo Dev in Jira became Jira Coding Agent, Copilot coding agent became cloud agent, Aardvark became Codex Security, Amazon's Kiro mandate was reversed, Spec Kit, OpenSpec and BMAD changed major version or command syntax, and Doctolib's in-house configuration tool was made useless by a native feature. The organizations that get through this pace isolate the tool behind their own layer (Spotify's CLI, which let it switch agents several times, AI-DLC's split between `core/` and `harness/`, proxies at Shopify, Zalando and Monzo to switch models) and rely on the open formats carried by the Agentic AI Foundation (AGENTS.md, MCP, Agent Skills). Distribution has its limits too: at Zalando, users who do not update their configuration only catch up when blocked, Intercom goes through IT for lack of a reliable native mechanism, and a repository template only updates new repositories ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html); [Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/curated-shared-instructions-for-software-teams)).

**The ticket is untrusted input, and an agent that writes to Jira acts on a shared surface.** The content of a ticket or a PR can carry a prompt injection: Anthropic advises against running its security review on untrusted PRs, and GitHub strips hidden characters and HTML comments from issues ([GitHub](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code-security-review); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/cloud-agent/risks-and-mitigations)). Symphony keeps the Linear token in the orchestrator, Jira Coding Agent cannot delete anything, Stripe and Monzo cut network egress from their sandboxes, and Meta's SEV1 incident came from an agent that posted on its own to an internal forum ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/open-source-codex-orchestration-symphony); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/18/meta-is-having-trouble-with-rogue-ai-agents)). For a framework that reads and writes in Jira, Confluence, the repository and production, the sources place credentials and side effects on the side of the orchestrator or the platform rather than the agent, and call for an audit trail of agent actions, which Microsoft, in its governance toolkit, ties to the OWASP Top 10 for agentic applications published in December 2025 ([Microsoft](https://microsoft.github.io/agent-governance-toolkit/compliance/owasp-agentic-top10-architecture/)).

**The default interaction mode decides what juniors learn.** Delegating code writing makes comprehension drop, asking conceptual questions preserves it, and supervision requires the skills that delegation erodes ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic)). A framework that maximizes delegation for everyone makes, for junior profiles, a trade-off between output and learning that the sources document without settling.

**The sources do not support claiming that a uniform framework works.** No organization publishes a complete ticket-to-deployment chain with its change failure rate; no controlled study compares Spec Kit, BMAD, Kiro or AI-DLC with an agent without a method; no source compares, within the same company, a central model and a model left to teams; no study establishes the optimal size of a shared base convention versus team rules, beyond the AGENTS.md benchmark limited to Python; most published volumes come from vendors or executives. The documented mandated rollouts concern tools or usage targets, never a workflow, and the best described one, Amazon's, has been relaxed. A framework of this kind will therefore have to produce the evidence of its effect itself, with a baseline and a comparison between teams, which the fixtures and the benchmark already present in harness-ai make possible.
